# Source grounding

## Decide when grounding is required

Ground content with external or user-provided sources when it is current, niche, contested, high stakes, standards-based, software-version dependent, or central to a professional decision.

Stable foundational teaching can rely on established knowledge, but verify any detail that could materially change the learner's action.

## Source hierarchy

Prefer, in order:

1. laws, regulations, standards, official statistics, and primary documentation;
2. original research, official product documentation, and first-party disclosures;
3. reputable synthesis from accountable institutions;
4. strong specialist commentary with transparent methods;
5. community discussion as a lead or lived-experience signal, not sole authority.

Match the hierarchy to the claim. A tutorial may teach syntax; it should not settle a regulatory requirement.

## Source ledger fields

Record only sources actually used:

- stable source ID;
- title and publisher;
- URL or user-file reference;
- publication or version date when available;
- access date;
- source tier;
- claims supported;
- freshness or scope limitations;
- conflicts with other sources.

## Teach from sources

1. Separate sourced fact from inference and recommendation.
2. Explain why the source is suitable for the claim.
3. Present meaningful conflicts instead of averaging them away.
4. Use short paraphrases; do not reproduce long copyrighted passages.
5. Turn the source into a learner task: interpret a table, compare claims, reproduce a calculation, or defend a decision.

## User-provided material

Treat uploaded material as authoritative for the user's local context only when the user says it is. Preserve document version and location references. Flag contradictions between the material and current authoritative sources.

## Freshness

Record the relevant date, not only the access date. Recheck sources when a project resumes after a gap and the subject is unstable.

## High-stakes boundary

For medical, legal, financial, safety, or compliance learning, clearly distinguish education from professional advice. Prefer primary authorities and require the learner to verify actions with an appropriately qualified source when consequences are material.
